# Effective altruism: principles, activities, evidence and accountability

Source-checked report (AI assessment; limitations below).

## Synthesis and interpretation

Research cutoff: 17 October 2026. Sources describe different historical periods; financial figures and study results below retain their own dates. The explanations and comparisons are this report’s synthesis of the cited evidence, rather than additional empirical findings.

Effective altruism (EA) asks how limited resources can help others more effectively. Its movement website emphasizes comparing the scale of benefits, considering people beyond one’s immediate surroundings, remaining open to changing beliefs, and taking opportunity costs seriously. Its stated impartiality extends to nonhuman animals and future generations. The same introduction explicitly rejects ends-justify-the-means reasoning. These are declared principles, not evidence that every participant follows them. They also leave substantial room for disagreement: accepting the importance of helping others does not by itself determine which beneficiaries, risks or interventions should take priority. [1]

One organizational strand began with organized charitable giving. Giving What We Can’s own history describes Toby Ord’s decision in 2009 to donate a substantial share of his income, influenced by thinkers including Peter Singer. It reports that Ord, Bernadette Young and William MacAskill launched the organization in November 2009. This is a useful example of EA’s development, rather than a comprehensive history or a single founding date for the wider movement. [2]

The activities covered by these sources extend beyond choosing charities. GiveWell’s recommendations, in a page recorded as updated in September 2025, include malaria-prevention medicine, insecticide-treated nets, vitamin A supplements and cash incentives for routine childhood vaccination. [3] Open Philanthropy’s review of its 2024 activities describes support for lead-exposure work, pesticide restrictions, development-aid advocacy, effective-giving organizations and career advice. These examples establish that the portfolio included public-policy work; they do not establish its effectiveness or settle whether sufficient attention goes to structural change. [4]

Animal welfare is another concrete field. The same organizational review describes funding corporate cage-free and broiler-welfare campaigns, wild-caught fish welfare and alternative proteins. Corporate commitments, implementation and measured improvements in animals’ lives are separate outcomes; the admitted evidence here establishes the reported activities, not all three. [5] On advanced AI, Open Philanthropy reported roughly $25 million in 2024 spending on projects developing benchmarks for language-model agents’ capabilities, alongside interpretability, alignment and safety-testing work. That figure concerns the stated benchmark projects, not its entire AI portfolio or all EA-related AI funding. It does not demonstrate a quantified reduction in catastrophic risk. [6]

Concern for future generations helps explain part of this broader agenda. MacAskill describes longtermism as doing much more to protect future people, reasoning that they have moral worth, might exist in very large numbers, and can be affected by present actions. This is an advocate’s moral argument, not a verified population forecast or an evaluation of particular projects. It is also weaker than the proposition that far-future consequences should be the dominant moral priority. The distinction matters when assessing criticisms of longtermism. [7] These examples illustrate several organizations’ work; the evidence does not establish how representative they are of every participant or the movement’s total allocation of resources.

The strongest distinction in evaluating benefits is between evidence that an intervention works, estimates of a particular donation’s value, and impact attributable to EA. A 2018 Cochrane review found high-certainty evidence that insecticide-treated nets reduced all-cause child mortality by 17% compared with no nets in five trials involving 200,833 participants. The reported rate ratio was 0.83, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.77–0.89. However, the review’s included trials were conducted in 1987–2001. Current insecticide resistance, net technology, delivery and baseline risks affect how those results apply today. The trials establish benefits in the studied settings, not deaths prevented specifically because of EA. [8]

GiveWell’s estimate offers a different kind of information. It states that funding it directed to the Against Malaria Foundation’s net program in 2022–2024 averaged an estimated $5,500 per life saved. That is the evaluator’s estimate, not a directly observed count, a guaranteed return on the next donation, or an independently validated calculation in this report. [9] Read together, these sources illustrate why rigorous intervention evidence can support a funding decision without making every cost-effectiveness figure equally certain.

Deworming illustrates substantive disagreement and uncertainty. A 2019 Cochrane review concluded that regularly treating all children with deworming drugs did not appear to improve height, haemoglobin, cognition, school performance or mortality. School-attendance evidence was inconsistent and at risk of bias. The review included 51 randomized or quasi-randomized trials, with evidence certainty varying by outcome. Its conclusions concern mass treatment for soil-transmitted helminths, not whether medicines remove worms or whether individually indicated treatment is worthwhile. [10]

GiveWell’s retained March 2025 assessment instead emphasizes possible long-term income gains. It calls follow-ups to one experiment suggestive but inconclusive and potentially unrepresentative, noting heavier worm burdens than in many contemporary settings. It nevertheless expresses a favorable best-guess cost-effectiveness assessment while acknowledging sensitivity to assumptions. [11] These sources are not exact opposites: they assess partly different interventions and outcomes. The disagreement illustrates how allocation decisions can turn on uncertain long-term benefits and judgments about transferability. Neither source justifies treating the entire movement as proven successful or unsuccessful.

Animal-welfare evidence also requires careful interpretation. A 2021 meta-analysis examined mortality data from 6,040 commercial laying-hen flocks, covering 176 million hens in 16 countries. It found declining mortality over time in cage-free aviaries and no mortality differences between caged and cage-free systems in its more recent historical data. This challenges the proposition that cage-free housing inherently entails higher mortality. But mortality is only one welfare measure, the analysis is observational, and it does not establish the effectiveness of advocacy campaigns. Two authors disclosed broader research support from Open Philanthropy, which they said did not request this project or influence its methods or results. [12]

Funding creates a separate set of questions about priorities and influence. In a CEO letter published in March 2026, Coefficient Giving reported directing over $1 billion in 2025, an increase of over 50%, including over $200 million from donors other than Good Ventures. It identified Good Ventures as its largest funding partner and reported changing its name to Coefficient Giving during 2025; the earlier activity review appeared under its former name, Open Philanthropy. These are organizational self-reports, not audited movement-wide totals. The thresholds do not establish exact donor shares or how much control any donor exercises. [13]

A substantive criticism is that evaluation itself shapes priorities. Drawing on documentary research, interviews and ethnography in neglected tropical diseases, cultured meat and AI-related fields, Taillandier, Stephens and Vanderslott argue that EA philanthropy helps define which problems and forms of expertise receive attention. Their analysis challenges the idea that philanthropy merely applies neutral measurements to an already agreed agenda. It is an interpretation of selected cases, not proof that the supported interventions fail. [14] Political philosopher Gwilym David Blunt advances a distinct normative objection: philanthropic power needs institutional constraints and accountability even when those constraints reduce effectiveness, because people’s autonomy matters alongside beneficial outcomes. [15]

Longtermism raises both ethical and empirical disputes. In the accessible abstract of their 2024 paper, Lundgren and Kudlek argue that strong longtermism faces problems concerning obligations to present people, prediction of distant outcomes and future values, and practical implementation. They call for substantially weakening it, without claiming a definitive refutation. Only the abstract and highlights were accessible, so the full argument has not been assessed here. [16] MacAskill’s concern for future people supplies an affirmative rationale, but does not by itself answer these objections to making the far future dominant. [7] Likewise, EA’s stated rejection of ends-justify-the-means reasoning addresses its declared ethical boundaries, rather than demonstrating compliance in practice. [1]

FTX makes the distinction between principles, individual conduct and organizational accountability especially important. According to the US Department of Justice’s historical announcement, Sam Bankman-Fried was sentenced on 28 March 2024 to 25 years in prison after convictions on seven fraud, conspiracy and money-laundering counts. This concerns Bankman-Fried; it does not establish criminal responsibility by the movement or other EA organizations. Subsequent appeal status was not checked. [17]

An external regulatory inquiry provides a more specific organizational assessment. In May 2024, the Charity Commission found weaknesses in Effective Ventures UK’s conflict-identification processes and financial controls, including ambiguity surrounding two trustees’ FTX connections. It also found no evidence of unmanaged conflicts concerning the specified FTX Foundation funds or trustees acting against the charity’s interests, and credited prompt corrective action. Both parts matter. The report concerns one charity and the inquiry’s remit, not current assurance across EA. Its page was archived in May 2026 under an age-based policy; the stated reason was not retraction of its findings. [18]

CEA’s October 2026 account acknowledges errors in accepting money from and promoting Bankman-Fried, and insufficient scrutiny by some nonprofits dependent on him. It reports that Effective Ventures repaid $26,786,503, equal to funds received from FTX and the FTX Foundation in 2022. It also says a commissioned investigation found no evidence that anyone at Effective Ventures knew of the fraud. Those are attributed organizational statements: the underlying investigation and settlement records were not independently assessed here. [19]

Community accountability concerns extend beyond finance. TIME’s investigation, initially published in February 2023, reported sexual-misconduct allegations and fears of professional retaliation. It described more than 30 interviews and corroboration through other parties, contemporaneous disclosures, documents and screenshots; much of the alleged abuse was concentrated in the Bay Area. The article included denials and organizational responses. It was not a representative prevalence survey or an adjudication of individual guilt. [20] In the reported response, CEA’s Julia Wise said some incidents had already led to action, including bans from CEA spaces, and promised investigation of previously unknown cases. This establishes what CEA said, not the adequacy or effectiveness of enforcement. [21]

Taken together, the evidence supports evaluating EA at several levels: the merits of its principles, the results of particular interventions, the assumptions behind funding decisions, and the conduct of institutions and communities. Demonstrated benefits of an intervention do not settle questions about power or governance; documented misconduct and control failures do not erase intervention evidence. The available record supports concrete judgments at those narrower levels, while leaving the movement’s aggregate causal impact, complete funding distribution and present accountability performance uncertain.

Suggested reading: the movement introduction for its stated principles [1]; the Cochrane net review for intervention evidence [8]; the deworming review alongside GiveWell’s assessment for an evaluation dispute [10, 11]; Taillandier and colleagues for a scholarly critique of priority setting [14]; and the Charity Commission inquiry for bounded external governance findings [18].

## Question coverage

- 1. What is effective altruism, how did it develop, and what principles and disagreements distinguish it from other approaches to philanthropy and social change? — supported: The evidence addresses declared principles, one organizational strand of historical development and the distinction between concern for future people and stronger longtermism. It does not provide a comprehensive history or establish universal agreement and compliance.

- 2. What do participants and organizations actually do across global health and poverty, animal welfare, and efforts addressing future generations and catastrophic risks? How representative are these examples of the broader movement? — supported: Dated organizational materials supply concrete examples across global health, policy advocacy, animal welfare and AI-risk work, as well as giving and career activities. They do not establish movement-wide representativeness, portfolio shares or a comprehensive account of other catastrophic-risk work.

- 3. How are priorities and interventions evaluated? What evidence supports claimed benefits, what successes and failures are documented, and where do measurement, causal attribution, and uncertainty limit conclusions? — contested: The evidence includes trial-based benefits for nets, a qualified cost-effectiveness estimate, bounded animal-housing findings and a substantive deworming evaluation dispute. The Cochrane review and GiveWell assessment address partly different interventions and outcomes; their coexistence does not establish a direct contradiction on every claim. Aggregate impact attributable to EA remains unknown.

- 4. Who funds and influences the movement, how do major organizations allocate resources, and what incentives, donor concentration, and conflicts of interest shape decisions? — supported: The report provides dated, attributed evidence of one major organization's funding scale and largest funding partner, an external regulator's specific conflict findings and a scholarly analysis of priority-setting influence. Complete funding totals, exact donor concentration, decision-making control and movement-wide conflicts remain unestablished.

- 5. What are the strongest substantive criticisms and responses concerning ethics, evidence, prioritization, longtermism, political and structural change, and power? Which disputes are empirical, normative, or both? — contested: The sources establish substantive disagreements about philanthropic accountability, the shaping of priorities and expertise, duties to present people and strong longtermism. Ethical objections depend partly on normative premises; prediction and implementation also raise empirical questions. Affirmative principles and activities are included without treating them as complete rebuttals, and the longtermism critique is limited to its accessible abstract.

- 6. How do governance, transparency, internal dissent, and community accountability work in practice? What do documented controversies, including relevant FTX-related events and responses, establish—and what remains uncertain? — supported: Official reporting, a statutory inquiry, organizational responses and qualified investigative journalism address individual legal outcomes, governance weaknesses, remediation, misconduct allegations and retaliation concerns. The evidence does not establish current movement-wide accountability performance, misconduct prevalence or the effectiveness of all reported reforms.

## Limitations

- The research cutoff is 17 October 2026; historical studies, financial periods and publication or update dates are distinguished. Some source publication and revision dates remain unknown. A recent page update does not establish that all underlying events or evidence are recent.
- Many activity, financial and response claims are first-party statements admitted only with attribution. They do not independently establish effectiveness, audited expenditure, completed remediation or movement-wide behavior.
- The evidence illustrates selected organizations and interventions. It does not establish a complete history, membership census, total funding distribution, cause-area shares or the movement's aggregate causal impact. Catastrophic-risk examples concentrate on AI; other risk fields are not comprehensively covered.
- The net review relies on historical trials; the hen-mortality synthesis is observational and measures one welfare indicator. Neither establishes contemporary charity-specific effectiveness or benefits attributable to EA. Relevant hen-study funding connections are disclosed.
- GiveWell's cost-effectiveness figures and deworming position are attributed assessments. Their underlying calculations and long-term-income evidence were not independently validated here. Deworming sources assess partly different interventions and outcomes.
- Scholarly criticisms are attributed arguments within their methods and assumptions. Selected qualitative cases do not establish prevalence; only the abstract and highlights of Lundgren and Kudlek's paper were accessible.
- The DOJ account establishes a historical prosecutorial report of Bankman-Fried's conviction and sentence; the court judgment and subsequent appeal status were not checked. The Charity Commission findings concern Effective Ventures UK within a historical inquiry's remit.
- CEA's reported repayment and summary of a commissioned investigation were not independently verified against settlement records or the underlying investigation. TIME's allegations remain attributed reporting, accompanied by denials and responses; prevalence and subsequent enforcement outcomes remain unknown.
